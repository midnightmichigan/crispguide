The accessory rule: buy for meals, not for the drawer

The accessory market around air fryers is enormous — and enormously mixed. Somewhere between the fryer itself and the third drawer of forgotten kitchen gadgets lies a simple test that sorts everything in this guide: does this accessory unlock a meal you'd otherwise cook badly or not at all? If yes, it earns its place. If it merely duplicates what the basket already does, slightly fancier, it doesn't.

This roundup applies that test to every major accessory category using three evidence sources: published specifications (materials, temperature ratings, compatibility), manufacturer care guidance (what you're allowed to put in the fryer), and aggregate buyer-feedback patterns — the recurring praises and the recurring regrets visible across large volumes of verified ratings. The verdicts name which profiles benefit, and which should pass.

Compatibility first: every accessory below must fit your specific model — basket shape and interior height vary even at equal quart ratings. Measure your basket's floor and height, check the accessory's listed dimensions, and confirm its temperature rating meets or exceeds your fryer's maximum (typically 400–450°F). Silicone, metal, and glass all behave differently; the details follow.

Worth buying

1. Instant-read thermometer — the single best purchase

Air fryer recipes run on estimates; safe meat is a measurement. The USDA's published minimum internal temperatures (165°F poultry, 160°F ground meat, 145°F whole cuts and fish) don't care how crispy the outside looks. An instant-read thermometer — not a probe you leave in, an instant-read you check with — converts every meat cook from a gamble into a known result. It's also the cheapest accessory in the category and the only one our beginner's guide calls essential rather than optional.

2. Perforated parchment liners — the cleanup shortcut that actually works

Plain basket liners block airflow and ruin crisping, but perforated parchment liners (holes punched for circulation) are the designed solution: they catch drips and prevent sticking while leaving the air path mostly open. They genuinely reduce basket scrubbing for sticky foods — marinated wings, sugary glazes, cheese-dusted anything. The safety rules from manufacturer guidance apply: always weigh liners down with food (a loose liner can lift into the heating element), never add one during preheat without food on it, and never reuse a scorched or torn liner. Measure to your basket shape — round liners in square baskets leave gaps, and vice versa.

3. Oil mister (non-aerosol) — the coating-protection play

Multiple manufacturer care guides warn against aerosol cooking sprays: propellants and additives like lecithin deposit a gummy film that degrades nonstick coatings over time. A refillable pump mister loaded with your own oil gives the same even coating — which helps browning and seasoning adhesion — without the chemistry that ruins baskets. The behavioral evidence supports it: nonstick longevity complaints concentrate around households that spray from a can. Fill it with a high-smoke-point oil (avocado, grapeseed, refined canola) given fryer temperatures near 400°F.

4. Metal skewer set / kebab rack — the second-layer unlock

For households that cook kebabs, satay, or shrimp skewers, a stand-up skewer rack genuinely expands what a basket can do: it holds food upright so hot air hits all sides, effectively adding a second cooking layer to the cavity. Stainless steel sets are dishwasher-safe and last indefinitely. Worth it if kebabs are a recurring meal in your house; dead weight if you've made skewers twice in your life.

5. Small bake pan (for models that support it) — the dessert and egg unlock

A small metal or silicone pan that fits your basket turns the fryer into a mini oven for items the basket can't hold: baked eggs, small frittatas, bread pudding, lava cakes. The key word is small — it must fit with airflow around it, and silicone pans need a rigid support underneath when lifting. Check your manual's guidance on oven-safe accessories (metal and silicone rated above your max temperature are generally fine; glass needs thermal-shock awareness). Worth it if you'd bake small things weekly; skip if dessert happens elsewhere.

Situational: worth it for some, skip for most

Rack / grill inserts

A raised rack adds a second layer for jerky or dehydrating, and it helps for high-volume small items. But stacking layers always cooks the top layer faster — manufacturer guidance and physics agree the top gets the hottest air — so you trade convenience for attention: plan on a mid-cook swap. Good for dehydrators and jerky-makers; unnecessary for everyday fryers.

Silicone egg molds and muffin cups

They work — egg bites and mini frittatas come out well — but they're a single-purpose purchase. The small metal bake pan above does the same jobs with more versatility. Buy only if you already make these foods regularly.

Tongs with silicone tips

Useful and cheap, but check the drawer first: many households already own exactly the right tool. Silicone tips protect nonstick coatings, which is the whole point — metal tongs on a nonstick basket is how coatings die young.

Dehydrating racks (tower-style)

If your model has a dehydrate function and you make jerky or dried fruit in volume, these multiply your per-run output. If the dehydrate function is a menu curiosity you'll use twice, the racks are shelf clutter.

Skip: landfill in waiting

Giant "100-piece" accessory kits

The most common regret pattern in accessory feedback: the bundle where you use three items and store ninety-seven. Bundles routinely include duplicate tools, wrong-sized liners, and single-purpose gadgets priced to look like a deal. Buy the three items on the "worth buying" list individually — you'll spend less than the kit and own nothing you don't use.

Non-perforated basket liners

Anything that sheets over the entire basket floor blocks the airflow that makes air frying work. Solid foil or paper liners turn your fryer into a weak oven and steam the bottom of the food. If cleanup is the goal, use perforated liners and accept the small compromise.

Aerosol spray oils

Not an accessory in the catalog sense, but the most consequential accessory-adjacent purchase: the propellants and additives in many aerosol sprays build the residue that shortens nonstick basket life. The pump mister above is the replacement.

Fryer-specific "cookbooks" bundled with accessories

Usually thin, generic, and written to the lowest-common-denominator fryer. The temperature-zone table and conversion rules in our beginner's guide — plus your model's manual — cover the same ground more accurately and for free.

Countertop fryer "covers"

Dust covers marketed for daily-use appliances are a fire-hazard-adjacent purchase: a cover left on when the machine is turned on melts, smokes, or worse. If the fryer lives on the counter, it doesn't need a cover; if it's stored, a cabinet is the cover. And never cover ventilation areas during operation — every manual says this, and the caution is worth repeating.

The minimum useful kit, summarized

For most households, the entire accessory purchase that pays for itself is three items: an instant-read thermometer (safety and confidence), perforated parchment liners in your basket's shape (cleanup), and a pump oil mister (browning without killing the nonstick). Everything beyond that follows the meals-you-actually-cook rule: kebabs get a skewer rack, bakers get a small pan, jerky-makers get racks. Buy for the menu, not the machine.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use any oven-safe dish in my air fryer?

Mostly yes, with constraints: the dish must fit with airflow around it, be rated above your fryer's max temperature, and be liftable safely when hot. Metal and high-heat silicone are the safest bets. Avoid thermal shock with glass (no cold glass into a hot fryer), and check your manual — some models restrict certain materials.

Do I need accessories at all?

Honestly, no — the fryer works out of the box for the core foods. Accessories add convenience (liners), longevity (mister), and safety (thermometer). Start cooking, note the friction points, then buy exactly what addresses them.

Will liners really not ruin crisping?

Perforated liners reduce crisping only marginally because the holes preserve airflow. Solid liners are a different story — they block air and should be avoided for crisp foods.
